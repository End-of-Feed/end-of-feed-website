What it's doing to your brain

It drains you even when it's off

Attention is limited. Your phone keeps taking a slice of it, whether you pick it up or not.

Your brainpower
100%Phone face-down on the desk. Silent. Still draining.

The "brain drain" experiment

Researchers at the University of Texas gave nearly 800 people tests that need full concentration. Some left their phone face-down on the desk, some kept it in a pocket or bag, and some left it in another room. Phones were silenced. The closer and more visible the phone, the lower people scored on working memory and problem-solving, even though they felt fully focused.3

Whether the phone was on or off made no difference. Part of the brain was busy not picking it up. The people who felt most dependent on their phones lost the most.3

47 seconds

UC Irvine researcher Gloria Mark has tracked how long people stay on one screen before switching. In 2004 it was about two and a half minutes. In her recent data it's about 47 seconds.4

~25 minutes

When an interruption pulls you away from a task, it takes on average over 20 minutes to get back to it, usually after drifting through other tasks first.4 Every buzz has a cost far longer than the glance.

The good news

In a 2025 study, people who blocked mobile internet on their phones for two weeks improved on an attention test by about as much as reversing 10 years of age-related decline.5 The damage is not fixed.

Health effects

Your body keeps the score

Sleep, mood and your spine all register the hours. The research on cause and effect is still developing, but the patterns are consistent.

Weight on your neck
5 kg Head tilt: 0°

Head upright. About 5 kg on your neck.

Illustration of Hansraj's calculations below

Text neck

An adult head weighs about 5 kg. Tilt it forward to look at a phone and the load on your neck climbs fast. Spine surgeon Kenneth Hansraj calculated the force at each angle. At a typical texting posture of 60°, it's about 27 kg, roughly the weight of an eight-year-old child.6

Hansraj estimated this adds up to 700 to 1,400 hours a year of extra stress on the neck. His fix is simple: lift the phone and move your eyes down, not your head.6

Sleep

A four-month study using sleep-tracking rings found that phone use in bed meant taking longer to fall asleep, more time awake in the night, a higher heart rate and lower heart-rate variability.7

Night mode is not the fix. In a BYU study, people using Night Shift slept no better than people using their phone normally. Only putting the phone away for the last hour helped, and only for those already getting about seven hours.8 The problem is the scrolling, not just the light.

Mood

Across studies, problematic phone use is consistently linked with depression, with medium-sized effects, and with anxiety, with smaller ones.9 A review of studies on young people found roughly 10 to 30% met a threshold for problematic use, which was associated with more depression, anxiety, stress and poor sleep.10

Straight talk

Most of this research shows a link, not proof that phones cause the harm. Low mood can drive more scrolling too. The strongest evidence that cutting back helps comes from experiments like the two-week block further down.

Unpredictable rewards

In the 1950s, B.F. Skinner found that animals pressed a lever hardest when food arrived unpredictably. A reward that might come is more compelling than one that always does. Behaviour trained this way is also the hardest to stop.11

Your feed works the same way. Refresh, and maybe there's something good. Former Google design ethicist Tristan Harris told the US Senate that refreshing a feed works like pulling a slot machine lever.12

Dopamine is about wanting

Dopamine is less a pleasure chemical than an anticipation chemical. It spikes in the moment before the reward, when you don't yet know what you'll get.12 That's why checking feels urgent and the result often feels flat.

The loop

Buzz or boredom→Check→Maybe a reward→Habit gets stronger↺

Researchers at Nottingham Trent found people checked their phones twice as often as they thought, mostly in bursts under 30 seconds.13 Much of it happens on autopilot.

Other hooks

Red badges that nag until cleared. Feeds with no bottom. Likes that arrive on their own schedule. Autoplay. Streaks you don't want to break. Each one removes a natural stopping point.

Your relationships

The person across the table

There's a word for ignoring someone in front of you to look at your phone: phubbing.

Connection

Full attention. Eye contact.

Illustration of the Baylor phubbing research below

46% phubbed

In Baylor University research, 46% of adults said their partner had phubbed them, and 22% said it had caused conflict.14

A chain reaction

The researchers traced a path: feeling phubbed led to conflict, then lower relationship satisfaction, then lower life satisfaction and higher depression.14 Later studies found phubbing leaves people feeling deprived of affection, which also lowers satisfaction.15

Just having it out

People in the study counted keeping a phone in sight or in hand during a conversation as snubbing.14 The phone on the table at dinner says something even when it's silent.

What helps

Take it back

You don't need to throw the phone in the sea. Change what's around it, so the easy choice is the good one. Flip a switch to see how.

Settings0 of 6 switched on

Out of sight is the only setting that fully removed the brain-drain effect in the Texas study.3

How: Pick one room the phone lives in during focused work, meals and family time.

Phone use in bed is what hurt sleep in the ring-tracking study.7

How: Buy a cheap alarm clock and charge the phone in another room overnight.

No buzz, no cue to check.

The 14-day challenge

No feeds. No scrolling. No browsing on the go. Your phone goes back to being a phone, and you get fourteen days to find out what you do with the time.

From a 2025 study of 467 people who blocked mobile internet on their phones for two weeks, published in PNAS Nexus. They spent the time with people face to face, exercising and outdoors.5

The rules

  • Turn off mobile data and Wi-Fi on your phone, or use a blocking app like Freedom to do it for you.
  • Calls and texts stay on. You can still be reached.
  • Need the internet? Use a laptop or computer, sitting down, on purpose.
  • Tell someone you're doing it, so they know why you've gone quiet.
  • Track it on paper, not on a screen. Tick one box every night.
  • Slip up? Don't start over. Pick it back up and keep going.
